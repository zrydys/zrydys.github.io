## The Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT)

The Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT) creates a centralized international patent-filing process. It does not grant a worldwide patent. Instead, it lets an applicant file one international application and postpone most country-by-country patent work while assessing commercial prospects and receiving an international search and written opinion.

## 1. Who may file

You generally may file a PCT application if you are:

- A national or resident of a PCT contracting state; or
- A company or other legal entity established in such a state.

The application may be filed with:

- A national or regional patent office acting as a receiving Office;
- The International Bureau of WIPO; or
- Another authorized receiving Office, depending on eligibility and filing method.

## 2. The basic PCT procedure

### Step 1: Establish a priority filing

Many applicants first file a national or regional patent application. That filing can establish a priority date under the Paris Convention.

A PCT application claiming that priority is normally filed within 12 months of the earliest priority application. Missing that deadline can seriously limit the ability to claim priority, although limited restoration mechanisms may exist under specific conditions.

### Step 2: File the international application

The application normally contains:

- Request;
- Description;
- One or more claims;
- Drawings, where required;
- Abstract;
- Priority information, if applicable;
- Applicant, inventor, and representative details;
- Required fees.

The application must satisfy formal requirements concerning format, language, signatures, drawings, claims, and other technical matters. The receiving Office checks formal compliance and assigns an international filing date if the minimum requirements are met.

### Step 3: International search

An authorized International Searching Authority (ISA) searches relevant prior art and prepares an international search report.

The report identifies published material that may be relevant to:

- Novelty;
- Inventive step or non-obviousness;
- Industrial applicability.

The ISA also issues a written opinion giving a preliminary, non-binding assessment of whether the claimed invention appears to satisfy those requirements.

The international search does not finally determine whether a patent will be granted. National or regional patent offices make the final decision.

### Step 4: International publication

WIPO generally publishes the international application shortly after 18 months from the priority date.

Publication usually includes:

- The application;
- Bibliographic information;
- The abstract;
- Drawings, where applicable;
- The international search report, if available by the publication date.

Publication makes the application available to the public and may have legal consequences under some national laws, although the details differ by country.

### Step 5: Optional supplementary search

An applicant may request a supplementary international search from an additional searching authority. This can be useful where:

- The invention uses specialized terminology;
- The original search authority had limited language coverage;
- Additional searching perspectives are valuable.

Additional fees and procedural requirements apply.

### Step 6: Optional international preliminary examination

The applicant may request international preliminary examination under Chapter II of the PCT. This can allow amendments and arguments to be considered before an international preliminary report is issued.

The resulting report is still not a patent grant or a binding national examination decision. Its main value is helping the applicant evaluate and refine the application before entering national or regional phases.

### Step 7: Enter national or regional phases

The PCT international phase ends when the applicant proceeds before individual national or regional offices.

The ordinary deadline is generally:

- 30 months from the priority date under the national phase provisions; or
- 31 months in many jurisdictions, depending on the relevant office’s rules.

At this stage, the applicant usually must:

- File a national or regional-phase request;
- Pay national or regional fees;
- Provide translations;
- Appoint a local representative where required;
- Satisfy local formalities;
- Respond to examination requirements;
- Pay examination, designation, or other local fees.

The deadline, translation requirements, examination procedure, and available remedies vary by jurisdiction. WIPO’s country-specific PCT Applicant’s Guide is updated frequently and should be checked for the particular countries selected. WIPO1

## 3. The most important PCT rules

### Priority — PCT Article 8 and Rule 4.10

The applicant may claim priority from an earlier application. The international application must generally be filed within 12 months of that earlier filing to preserve the priority claim.

The priority document may need to be submitted or made available through an accepted digital system. Requirements and consequences for defects are governed by the PCT Rules and the applicable receiving Office.

### Filing date — PCT Article 11

An international filing date can be accorded when required minimum elements are present, including:

- An indication that the filing is intended to be an international application;
- Designation of at least one contracting state;
- The applicant’s name;
- A part that appears to be a description;
- A part that appears to be a claim.

If required elements are missing, the receiving Office may invite correction or refuse to accord the claimed filing date.

### Designations — PCT Rule 4.9

PCT applications generally make broad designations of contracting states. However, national law may affect whether a particular state or regional route is available for a particular type of protection.

Applicants should also check whether a state has made reservations or declarations affecting specific PCT provisions.

### Unity of invention — PCT Rule 13

An application should relate to one invention or a group of inventions linked by a single general inventive concept.

If the ISA considers that the application lacks unity, it may invite the applicant to pay additional search fees. The applicant may also be asked to identify which invention or group of inventions should be searched.

### Amendments under Article 19

After receiving the international search report, the applicant may usually file amended claims under PCT Article 19.

Key limitations include:

- The amendment is generally limited to the claims;
- It must be filed within the applicable time limit;
- It cannot add new subject matter;
- A statement explaining the amendments may accompany the amended claims.

### Amendments under Article 34

During international preliminary examination, amendments may generally be made to:

- Claims;
- Description;
- Drawings.

These amendments must remain within the disclosure of the application as originally filed. New matter cannot be added.

### Rectification of obvious errors — PCT Rule 91

A request may be available to correct an obvious error in certain parts of the application or documents. Strict conditions and time limits apply, and not every correction qualifies.

### Restoration of rights and missed time limits

The PCT contains mechanisms concerning missed time limits, restoration of rights, and excuse of delays. These are not automatic and may depend on:

- The reason for the delay;
- Whether due care was taken;
- Whether the failure was unintentional;
- The office involved;
- The specific deadline and provision.

National phase offices may apply their own rules to missed national-phase deadlines. WIPO2

## 4. What the PCT does and does not do

The PCT can:

- Create one international filing route;
- Preserve a priority claim where requirements are met;
- Delay major national costs;
- Provide an international search report;
- Provide preliminary written assessments;
- Simplify initial administration across many jurisdictions.

The PCT cannot:

- Grant a worldwide patent;
- Replace national or regional patent offices;
- Guarantee that an invention is patentable;
- Eliminate national filing, translation, examination, or grant fees;
- Guarantee identical outcomes in different countries.

A central misconception is that a PCT application itself becomes an international patent. In reality, the applicant must eventually pursue protection separately before selected national or regional offices.

## 5. A practical timeline

|Time from earliest priority date|Typical action|
|---|---|
|Month 0|File first national or regional application|
|By month 12|File PCT application claiming priority|
|Shortly after filing|Formalities review and international search|
|Around month 16|Search report and written opinion may become available|
|Around month 18|International publication|
|Before national-phase deadline|Select countries, arrange translations and local counsel|
|Month 30 or 31, depending on jurisdiction|Enter national or regional phases|
|After entry|Local examination, amendments, responses, and grant procedures|

The exact deadline must be calculated for each designated jurisdiction. Some countries use 30 months, others 31 months, and exceptional national rules may apply.

## 6. Common strategic issues

Drafting: The original disclosure should fully support the protection later sought. New technical matter generally cannot be added after filing.

Claims: Broad claims may provide wider potential protection but can face more prior-art objections. Narrow claims may be easier to defend but can reduce commercial scope.

Search results: A negative search report does not automatically end the application. It can help identify weaknesses and guide amendments or decisions about national-phase entry.

Costs: The PCT delays many national costs; it does not eliminate them. Later expenses may include translations, national fees, local attorneys, examination fees, and maintenance fees.

Country selection: Entering every available country is often impractical. Selection should reflect commercial markets, manufacturing locations, competitors, licensing opportunities, and enforcement prospects.

Confidentiality: Before international publication, the application is generally treated as non-public within the PCT system, subject to applicable legal exceptions. Applicants should avoid public disclosure before the first filing because national laws differ on grace periods and prior disclosure.

The most useful way to analyze a PCT matter is to separate it into three layers: PCT international-phase rules, the rules of the receiving or searching Office, and the national or regional law of each country where protection will be pursued.
